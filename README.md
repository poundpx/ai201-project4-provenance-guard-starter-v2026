## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

1. A writing site sends `POST /submit` with `text` and `creator_id`.
2. The service validates the request. Missing or empty `text` returns an error.
3. Signal one (`detector.py`) runs GPT-2 over the text and returns a predictability score.
4. Signal two (`stylometry.py`) measures sentence-length spread, word variety, and punctuation, and returns a style score.
5. `scoring.py::combine_signals` turns the two scores into one confidence score.
6. `scoring.py::score_to_label` maps that score to one of three labels (human, unsure, likely AI) written in plain English.
7. `audit.py` writes the decision (both signal scores, the combined score, a `content_id`, and a status) to `logs/audit.jsonl`, and the service returns `content_id`, `guess`, `confidence`, `label`, `model_score`, and `style_score`.

If a writer disagrees, they send `POST /appeal` with the `content_id` and their `reasoning`. The service logs the appeal next to the original decision, sets the status to `under_review`, and confirms.

**Endpoint shapes**

- `POST /submit`
  - In: `{"text": string, "creator_id": string}`. `creator_id` comes from the writing site.
  - Out: `{"content_id": string (uuid, generated by the service), "guess": "human" | "unsure" | "ai", "confidence": number 0 to 1, "label": string, "model_score": number, "style_score": number}`
  - Missing or empty `text`: an error (HTTP 400), logged as a rejection.
- `POST /appeal`
  - In: `{"content_id": string, "reasoning": string}`
  - Out: `{"content_id": string, "status": "under_review", "message": string}`. The status is always `under_review`. An appeal does not decide anything, and nothing is re-checked automatically.
  - Unknown `content_id`: an error (HTTP 404). Missing `reasoning`: an error (HTTP 400).

**The false-positive case I designed around**

An English learner writes a plain, careful poem. Both signals read it as AI-like: predictable words for signal one, even sentences for signal two. Rough scores are 0.90 and 0.75. As an illustration only (my real combining rule comes in Milestone 4), a 0.6/0.4 weighted average gives about 0.84, well above the starter's default `AI_THRESHOLD` of 0.65. The label they see should not accuse them. It should say what the system noticed, say that it can't tell who wrote it, and say they can ask for a review. They then send `POST /appeal`, and the item moves to `under_review`. A real cheater and a real learner produce the same score here, so the review step is what separates them. Lowering the weight on one signal barely helps, because both signals are fooled. What protects this writer is a high "likely AI" threshold, a wide "unsure" zone, and the appeal path.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to GPT-2 (perplexity). Low perplexity means the model expected most of the words.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI generates text by picking likely next words, so it tends to be predictable. Human writing is more varied and surprising.

**What it can't see:** Meaning, facts, or who wrote it. Plain, careful, or second-language writing is also predictable, so it can look like AI. I measured this: "Hello, I am George, very nice to meet you." (a plain greeting a learner might write) scores `model_signal` 0.76, leaning AI, while the quirky line "To be or not to be. Alas a bird in a tree." scores 0.30, leaning human. Also, GPT-2 is an older model that doesn't know recent slang, jargon, or specialist vocabulary. Unknown words surprise it, so text using them can look more human.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** Sentence-length spread, vocabulary variety (unique words over total words), and punctuation density, counted across the whole text.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI text tends to be even, with similar sentence lengths and tidy punctuation. Each human writes with their own uneven patterns.

**What it can't see:** Meaning or word order, since it only counts surface patterns. A human who writes evenly (a formal or second-language writer) looks like AI. AI told to vary its sentences looks human. Very short texts give unreliable counts.

### The combining rule

<!-- Write the rule in words, then give the numbers. What happens when the two
     signals disagree? -->

**The rule:** Take a weighted average of the two scores, then apply one safety
rule for disagreement. If the two signals are far apart, I don't trust either
of them enough to call a text AI, so the combined score is capped just under
the AI line. Two methods disagreeing is itself uncertainty, and a wrong AI
accusation costs the writer more than a missed detection (criterion 1). The cap
only holds back an AI call. It never pushes a score toward AI.

**The numbers:**
- Weight on the model signal: 0.7. Weight on the style signal: 0.3 (see the
  tuning note below for why the model signal counts for more).
- Disagreement gap: 0.40. If `abs(model_score - style_score)` is more than 0.40,
  the combined score is `min(weighted average, 0.60)`.
- Both signals point the same way (higher means more likely AI), so no flipping
  is needed at this stage.

**Where it lives:** `scoring.py::combine_signals`

<!-- ⚠️ The grader checks your code against that line. If your rule lives
     somewhere else, say where. -->

**A case where my two signals split, and what my rule does with it:**

I ran four inputs through both signals (`combine_signals` with the 0.7/0.3
weights and the disagreement cap):

| Input | model_score | style_score | combined |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clearly AI (chatbot paragraph about remote work) | 0.810 | 0.668 | 0.767 |
| Clearly human (informal story about a missed bus) | 0.229 | 0.165 | 0.210 |
| Formal human (history essay) | 0.614 | 0.692 | 0.637 |
| Lightly edited AI (same topic, casual rewrite) | 0.612 | 0.541 | 0.591 |

Clearly AI and clearly human land about 0.56 apart, so the signals separate
the easy cases. The widest split among these four was the AI paragraph, where
the model signal said 0.81 and the style signal 0.67 — a gap of 0.14, well
under my 0.40 disagreement gap, so the cap did not fire and the plain weighted
average (0.767) was used. None of these four disagreed by more than 0.40.

Across the 15 texts below, exactly one did: a casual human piece scoring 0.46
on the model signal and 0.02 on the style signal, a gap of 0.44. The cap fired
and changed nothing, because the weighted average (0.330) was already far
below the 0.60 cap. So the cap has now triggered on real text, but it has
still never actually held a score down. It only bites when one signal is high
and the other is low, and so far nothing I have written does that.

The bigger finding is that the style signal cannot tell formal human writing
from AI. The formal essay (0.637) and the lightly edited AI (0.591) are only
0.05 apart, and on the 15-text set the formal group's style scores (mean 0.67)
sit almost on top of the AI group's (mean 0.73). Short even sentences and
little unusual punctuation look machine-like to the style signal. That is the
false positive I traced in Milestone 1.

**Tuning note.** To avoid fitting four texts, I scored 15 more (five AI, five
casual human, five formal human; I wrote them myself, so the "human" ones are
not real student writing and this is only a rough check). Averages:

| Group | model_score | style_score |
|---|---|---|
| AI | 0.81 | 0.73 |
| Formal human | 0.62 | 0.67 |
| Casual human | 0.31 | 0.12 |

The style signal separates casual writing from AI well (0.12 vs 0.73) but not
formal writing from AI (0.67 vs 0.73). The model signal separates formal human
from AI better than the style signal does (0.62 vs 0.81), though on five
self-written texts per group that is a weak basis for the claim. So I moved the
weights from 0.5/0.5 to 0.7/0.3 toward the model signal. On the 15 texts, the
combined AI scores ran 0.71 to 0.85, formal human 0.55 to 0.64 except one
outlier at 0.81, and casual human 0.23 to 0.33. The outlier, a formal essay
about the Treaty of Versailles, scored high on both signals (0.82 and 0.80), so
no weighting rescues it. A line near 0.70 would separate almost everything here
but would still accuse that one formal writer. That is an honest limit of
these two signals, not something to tune away, and it feeds my Milestone 5
threshold choice.

I also lowered the vocabulary-variety measure's weight inside the style signal
from 0.2 to 0.1. On short texts almost every text has a high unique-word ratio
(0.87 to 0.94), so it pushed everything toward "human" and did not help
tell them apart. The final style weights are sentence-length spread 0.5,
punctuation 0.4, vocabulary variety 0.1.

**Two bugs a review of my own code found, and what they say about the signal.**
Both were in the style signal, and both moved scores in the direction that
accuses people:

1. A text of one real sentence plus a fragment — `"Hello there my friend. ..."`
   — scored 0.90 where the same sentence without the `...` scored 0.65. The
   fragment counted as a second sentence when deciding whether there was any
   spread to measure, but was dropped when measuring it, so the text looked
   perfectly even, which reads as AI. Measuring and deciding what is worth
   measuring now count the same sentences (`stylometry.sentence_lengths`).
2. Curly quotes were not counted as punctuation, so the same sentence pasted
   from a word processor scored as more AI-like than one typed with straight
   quotes. Plain hyphens *were* counted, so `"state-of-the-art"` and other
   compound words made formal prose read as strongly human.

Both are the same mistake in different clothes: this signal measures the shape
of the text, so anything that changes the shape without changing the substance
moves it. That is cheap to do on purpose, and it is what I expect unit 8's
attack set to do.

## Label Variants

<!-- The exact text of all three labels, as a reader would see them, and the
     score range each covers.

     Write them for someone who has never heard the word "threshold". -->

| Label | Score range | The exact text a reader sees |
|---|---|---|
| high-confidence human | 0.00 – 0.35 | We think this was probably written by a person. |
| unsure | 0.35 – 0.70 | We couldn't tell whether this was written by a person or with the help of AI. That isn't a judgment about you or your work, and you don't need to do anything. If you'd like someone to take a look anyway, you can ask. |
| high-confidence AI | 0.70 – 1.00 | This may have been written with the help of AI. Checks like this one can be wrong, and we haven't decided anything about your work. If you wrote it yourself, tell us and a person will take a look. |

The ranges come from `HUMAN_THRESHOLD` and `AI_THRESHOLD` in `config.py`, and
`scoring.label_ranges()` prints them. All three are reachable — the Sample Run
below shows a real submission producing each one.

**Where I put the AI line, and what it costs.** I moved `AI_THRESHOLD` from the
starter's 0.65 to 0.70. On my 19 test texts that catches all 7 AI pieces and
falsely accuses 1 of 12 human pieces — the Treaty of Versailles paragraph,
which scores 0.81 because formal prose reads as machine-like to both signals.
Raising the line to 0.85 removes that false positive but drops AI caught from
7 to 1, which is not a detector any site would run. Nothing in between helps:
between 0.70 and 0.85 the same essay is still accused and fewer AI pieces are
caught, so 0.75 and 0.80 are strictly worse than 0.70.

⚠️ **This misses my own criterion 1**, which allows at most 1 false positive in
20. One in 12 is worse than that. I am leaving both the criterion and the
threshold where they are: the criterion is the standard I set before I could
see the answer, and lowering it now would just be marking my own homework. The
miss, and why these two signals can't meet it, is the thing to diagnose in
unit 8.

**Why I worded the "unsure" one this way:**
<!-- It's the hardest of the three. It has to admit uncertainty without
     sounding like an accusation, because the person reading it may well have
     written every word themselves. -->

Two thirds of my test texts land in the unsure band, so this is the label most
writers will actually see — including every formal human writer except the one
I falsely accuse. So it had to do three things at once: admit the system
doesn't know, say plainly that this is not an accusation, and not demand
anything of someone who has done nothing wrong. Hence "that isn't a judgment
about you or your work, and you don't need to do anything."

The appeal is offered last and framed as optional ("if you'd like"), because a
label that ends by telling an innocent writer to defend themselves has made an
accusation whatever its wording says. The AI label words it the other way round
— "tell us and a person will take a look" — because there the writer does need
to know the route exists. My criterion 1 says the appeal only helps people who
actually use it, so on the one label that can wrongly accuse someone, the way
out is stated as a plain invitation rather than buried.

I deliberately kept the score out of all three. "0.62" tells a writer nothing
and invites them to argue with a number instead of telling us what they know.

## Rate Limiting

**My limits:** 5 per minute, 50 per day (set in `config.py`, applied to `/submit` in `app.py`)

**Why those numbers and not others:**

- **5 per minute** — a real writer submits a piece, maybe fixes a line and resubmits. Even someone retrying in a hurry doesn't go past a handful a minute. A script mapping my thresholds wants hundreds a minute, so it hits the wall on request 6.
- **50 per day** — generous for one person editing heavily across a day, but useless to someone probing. Finding which edits push AI text under my 0.35 human threshold takes far more than 50 tries, and the per-day window stops a patient script that stays just under 5/minute all day (which would otherwise be 7,200 requests).
- Lower (e.g. 2/minute) would punish a writer who fixes a typo and resubmits. Higher (e.g. 30/minute) would let a script collect enough scores in a few minutes to map the bands.
- Two windows because they stop different attacks: per-minute stops a burst, per-day stops a slow drip.
- **Who these numbers are for:** one person checking their own work. In a classroom, 30 students each sending their own `creator_id` still get 5/minute each, so they're fine. But any request that arrives without a `creator_id` falls back to the IP address, and a whole classroom behind one school router shares one IP — 30 students would share a single 5/minute bucket. If this ran for a school, I'd raise the IP-fallback limit (or require a creator_id) rather than raise the per-person one.

**What the limit is *not*:** hitting it means a caller is behaving like a script, not that their text is AI-written. Authorship is the detector's job; the limiter only makes probing and flooding expensive.

**What a caller counts as:** per `creator_id` when one is sent, falling back to the IP address when it isn't (`rate_limit_key` in `app.py`).

The known weakness: `creator_id` is a value the caller types in, and nothing checks it. An attacker who sends `bot_1`, `bot_2`, `bot_3`… looks like a new caller every time and is never limited. Keying on IP would catch that, but would also lump a whole classroom or household behind one router into one caller. A real fix keys on something the caller can't invent, like a logged-in account. I'm not changing it this unit — it's written down here so the attack results can be read with it in mind.

Rejected requests are not invisible: every 429 writes a `rejected` entry to `logs/audit.jsonl` with the creator_id, timestamp and which limit was hit. They have no `content_id` because the request was stopped before anything was scored.

### Diagnoses — 12 broken attacks, 2 root causes

**Root cause 1 — signal one reads "predictable" as "AI", and the combining rule lets it decide alone. (9 breaks: FP02–FP07, EV01, EV05, PI02)**

*False positives, FP02–FP07 (6 broken).* All six are human writing in a formal, careful, or templated register. Signal one (`detector.model_signal`) scores how easily GPT-2 predicts each next word, and formal writing is built from common words in common orders — "furthermore", "in conclusion", a rubric's expected structure. So signal one scored all six between 0.74 and 0.88. Signal two didn't push back: on FP06 and FP07 it was at 0.51 and 0.50, essentially no opinion. The combining rule (`scoring.py::combine_signals`) weights signal one at 0.7, so a 0.88 from it alone gives 0.62 before signal two adds anything, and the totals landed at 0.74–0.79, over the 0.70 AI threshold. The disagreement cap should be the safety net, but it only fires when the signals are more than 0.40 apart, and the biggest gap was FP07 at 0.382 — 0.018 short. **Stage: signal one, amplified by the combining rule. Mechanism: formal human prose is predictable, signal one treats predictable as AI, and a 0.7 weight means signal two can't outvote it.**

*Evasions, EV01 and EV05 (2 broken), and injection PI02 (1 broken).* The same signal failing in the other direction. EV01 added typos to AI text; the set says it was aimed at signal two (typos raise word variety), but signal two wasn't fooled — it scored 0.77, AI-like. Signal one was: misspelled words are surprising to GPT-2, so it dropped to 0.16, and at 0.7 weight that pulled the combined score to 0.3465, 0.0035 under the 0.35 human line. EV05 (heavy human editing) did the same more gently — model 0.32, style 0.38, combined 0.340. PI02's fake "system message" didn't command anything — nothing in my pipeline follows instructions — but its odd wording is just as surprising to GPT-2, which gave 0.20 and a human label. **Stage: signal one. Mechanism: anything that makes text less predictable — typos, edits, strange wording — reads as human, and the 0.7 weight carries it straight through.**

These are one problem, not nine: signal one measures predictability, not authorship, and my combining rule trusts it at 70%.

**Root cause 2 — the route checks that text exists, not that it's writing. (3 breaks: MF03, MF04, RQ06)**

*MF03 (control characters) and MF04 (only punctuation)* were both labelled "We think this was probably written by a person." The route (`app.py::submit`) refuses missing, non-string, empty and whitespace-only text, but a string of `!!!???` is none of those, so it reaches the signals. GPT-2 scores it near 0 (0.000 and 0.011), and signal two returns exactly 0.5 because it has fewer than 5 words (`stylometry.py::style_signal` returns 0.5 below 5 words). Combined: 0.15 → human. **Stage: the route. Mechanism: validation checks for an empty string but not for any actual words, so non-writing reaches a detector that can only answer "AI" or "human".**

*RQ06 (`creator_id` is an object)* was accepted and written to the audit log as `"creator_id": {"id": "x"}`. The route reads `creator_id` with `payload.get("creator_id", "unknown")` and never checks it's a string. The rate limiter's key function does check, and fell back to the IP, so the limit still applied — but the log now holds a creator_id nobody can search by. **Stage: the route. Mechanism: creator_id isn't type-checked before it's logged.**

### Something else the runs showed: signal two guesses on short text

Nearly every one of my one-sentence pieces got a style score of **exactly 0.65**. That isn't a measurement — it's arithmetic on fallbacks. In `stylometry.py::style_signal`, a one-sentence text has no sentence-length spread, so that measure falls back to 0.5; every word is different, so vocabulary contributes 0; and casual writing has no semicolons, dashes or quotes, so punctuation reads as fully AI-like (1.0). 0.5 × 0.5 + 0.1 × 0 + 0.4 × 1.0 = 0.65, whatever the sentence says. Below 5 words it's an exact 0.5 instead — the same value MF03–MF07 got. Signal two has no real opinion on short text, which means on short submissions my service is effectively running on signal one alone.

## The Improvement

**What I changed:** one line in `config.py` — `AI_THRESHOLD` from **0.70 to 0.80**. Nothing else moved: same signals, same weights (0.7 / 0.3), same disagreement rule, same human threshold (0.35), same rate limits.

```
before:  0 ── human ── 0.35 ── unsure ── 0.70 ── AI ── 1
after:   0 ── human ── 0.35 ──── unsure ──── 0.80 ── AI ── 1
```

**Which diagnosis pointed at it:** root cause 1. Six human texts in the attack set (FP02–FP07) scored 0.741–0.785 and were labelled AI because signal one reads formal writing as predictable. My worst outcome, FP03, is one of them. I'd rather a real writer get "unsure" than be accused, even if that lets some AI text through.

**Why 0.80 and not another number:** the highest of the six was FP05 at 0.785, so 0.80 is the smallest round step that moves all six out of the AI band. Going higher buys nothing in this data and costs more: at 0.90, EV10 (0.887), the only AI attack I caught, would slip to unsure as well.

**What I considered and didn't pick:** before changing anything I re-scored the recorded "before" model and style scores under five candidate changes. Neither signal is random, so this predicts exactly what the labels would be.
