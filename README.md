## What This Does

<!-- The path a submission takes, from arriving to the label a reader sees.
     Six or seven steps — this is the trace you wrote in Milestone 1. -->

1. A writing site sends `POST /submit` with `text` and `creator_id`.
2. The service validates the request. Missing or empty `text` returns an error.
3. Signal one (`detector.py`) runs GPT-2 over the text and returns a predictability score.
4. Signal two (`stylometry.py`) measures sentence-length spread, word variety, and punctuation, and returns a style score.
5. `scoring.py::combine_signals` turns the two scores into one confidence score.
6. `scoring.py::score_to_label` maps that score to one of three labels (human, unsure, likely AI) written in plain English.
7. `audit.py` writes the decision (both signal scores, the combined score, a `content_id`, and a status) to `logs/audit.jsonl`, and the service returns `content_id`, `guess`, `confidence`, `label`, `model_score`, and `style_score`.

If a writer disagrees, they send `POST /appeal` with the `content_id` and their `reasoning`. The service logs the appeal next to the original decision, sets the status to `under_review`, and confirms.

**Endpoint shapes**

- `POST /submit`
  - In: `{"text": string, "creator_id": string}`. `creator_id` comes from the writing site.
  - Out: `{"content_id": string (uuid, generated by the service), "guess": "human" | "unsure" | "ai", "confidence": number 0 to 1, "label": string, "model_score": number, "style_score": number}`
  - Missing or empty `text`: an error (HTTP 400), logged as a rejection.
- `POST /appeal`
  - In: `{"content_id": string, "reasoning": string}`
  - Out: `{"content_id": string, "status": "under_review", "message": string}`. The status is always `under_review`. An appeal does not decide anything, and nothing is re-checked automatically.
  - Unknown `content_id`: an error (HTTP 404). Missing `reasoning`: an error (HTTP 400).

**The false-positive case I designed around**

An English learner writes a plain, careful poem. Both signals read it as AI-like: predictable words for signal one, even sentences for signal two. Rough scores are 0.90 and 0.75. As an illustration only (my real combining rule comes in Milestone 4), a 0.6/0.4 weighted average gives about 0.84, well above the starter's default `AI_THRESHOLD` of 0.65. The label they see should not accuse them. It should say what the system noticed, say that it can't tell who wrote it, and say they can ask for a review. They then send `POST /appeal`, and the item moves to `under_review`. A real cheater and a real learner produce the same score here, so the review step is what separates them. Lowering the weight on one signal barely helps, because both signals are fooled. What protects this writer is a high "likely AI" threshold, a wide "unsure" zone, and the appeal path.

## Signals and Scoring

<!-- For each signal: what property it measures, why that might differ between
     human and AI writing, and WHAT IT CAN'T SEE.

     The blind spot is not optional. If you can't name it you don't understand
     the signal yet — and unit 8's attack set is built out of blind spots. -->

### Signal one — the model signal

**What it measures:** How predictable the text is to GPT-2 (perplexity). Low perplexity means the model expected most of the words.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI generates text by picking likely next words, so it tends to be predictable. Human writing is more varied and surprising.

**What it can't see:** Meaning, facts, or who wrote it. Plain, careful, or second-language writing is also predictable, so it can look like AI. I measured this: "Hello, I am George, very nice to meet you." (a plain greeting a learner might write) scores `model_signal` 0.76, leaning AI, while the quirky line "To be or not to be. Alas a bird in a tree." scores 0.30, leaning human. Also, GPT-2 is an older model that doesn't know recent slang, jargon, or specialist vocabulary. Unknown words surprise it, so text using them can look more human.

### Signal two — the style signal

**What it measures:** Sentence-length spread, vocabulary variety (unique words over total words), and punctuation density, counted across the whole text.

**Why that might differ between human and AI writing:** AI text tends to be even, with similar sentence lengths and tidy punctuation. Each human writes with their own uneven patterns.

**What it can't see:** Meaning or word order, since it only counts surface patterns. A human who writes evenly (a formal or second-language writer) looks like AI. AI told to vary its sentences looks human. Very short texts give unreliable counts.
